**The Paradigm of Antiquity: Intuitive "Quantity of Matter" and the Primary Division of Properties**

The origins of the modern crisis of the concept of mass are rooted in classical antiquity. **1. Aristotle's Dogma: The False Division into "Light" and "Heavy"**

In Aristotle’s natural philosophy, which dominated human minds for nearly two millennia, the concept of a unified "mass" did not exist in principle.         
Aristotle divided the material world into elements possessing absolute intrinsic inclinations:    
•	Earth and Water possessed an inherent property of heaviness (the aspiration to fall toward the center of the Universe, the center of which was the center of the Earth).    
•	Air and Fire possessed an inherent property of lightness (the aspiration to rise upward).

Aristotle's primary error lay in the fact that he attributed these dynamic acts to the body itself. He sincerely believed that a stone falls because a desire to move toward its "natural place" is embedded within its nature. The medium (air or water) in his physics was viewed exclusively as a passive barrier that merely hinders the body from realizing its intrinsic aspiration. Aristotle completely inverted causality: instead of recognizing how a dense medium forces light air upward and presses a heavy stone downward, he declared this to be the inherent "magic" of the objects themselves.

**2. The Atomism of Democritus and Lucretius: A Step Toward Drag and a Fall Into the Void**

A progressive step against this backdrop was attempted by the atomists — Democritus, Leucippus, and later Epicurus and Lucretius Carus. They were the first to introduce the intuitive concept of the "quantity of matter." In their interpretations, the heaviness of a body depended directly on the number of atoms contained within the object. In his poem On the Nature of Things, Lucretius explicitly writes: "Why do we see that some things weigh more than others, though of equal size?" and answers that it is entirely a matter of the packing density of the atoms and the presence of void between them.

Here, the atomists came remarkably close to the idea of a structural sieve: they understood that weight depends on the number of fundamental building blocks. However, they immediately committed mental suicide by placing their atoms into an absolute void.

Antiquity left a heavy legacy to science. It cemented a false mental habit:
