## Chapter 26. THE MICHELSON EXPERIMENT AND WHY THE WRONG ETHER WAS SEARCHED FOR

At the end of the 19th century, physicists were convinced that light is a wave. And if it is a wave, there must be a medium in which it propagates. Sound propagates in air, waves propagate in water. Therefore, light must propagate in some universal medium called ether. The Earth moves around the Sun at about 30 km/s. Therefore, it moves through this ether. And if it moves, an “ether wind” should arise — just as a person running on a windless day feels an oncoming flow of air.

The logic was simple. If light propagates in ether, then its speed relative to the Earth should depend on direction. Against the “wind” — slightly slower. With the “wind” — slightly faster. The difference would be small, but measurable.

In 1887, the American physicists Albert Michelson and Edward Morley performed an extremely precise experiment. The device — an interferometer — split a beam of light into two perpendicular beams, sent them in different directions, and then recombined them. If the speed of light differed even slightly depending on direction, this would appear in the interference pattern — a characteristic set of alternating bright and dark fringes. The instrument was sensitive enough to detect even a tiny fraction of the expected difference.

The result shocked the scientific world. No difference was found. In any direction. At any orientation of the instrument. Light propagated at the same speed regardless of direction and regardless of the Earth’s motion.

The conclusion physics drew was: ether does not exist. There is no medium. Light propagates in a vacuum. This conclusion became a foundation of the special theory of relativity by Albert Einstein.

But here lies the fundamental mistake — not in the experiment, but in its interpretation. More precisely, in the assumption of what the ether should be.

Physicists of the 19th century imagined ether as something like a gas or a fluid — a rarefied medium through which the Earth moves like a ship through water, creating waves and encountering resistance. This is the ether Michelson was looking for. And this is the ether he did not find.

But aol space is structured fundamentally differently. It is not a rarefied gas in which the Earth floats. It is a densely packed aol—a medium that fills all space without a single gap. This is precisely why there is and cannot be any "ether wind." And most importantly, there are no gaps in the dense packing of aols; their number in any direction of the light beam is constant, determined by the rule "aol to aol," densely packed.

Now about the speed of light. In the aol model, light is not a flying particle and not a wave in a moving medium. It is the transmission of vibrations from aol to aol. The speed of this transmission is determined solely by the properties of the aols themselves: their stiffness and packing density. It does not depend on the motion of the source or on the direction of propagation.

When an atom of the source strikes a neighboring aol, a vibration arises. It is then transmitted along a chain of aols at a constant speed determined by the properties of the medium. The velocity of the source is neither added nor subtracted — because the source does not “throw” any object. It only initiates a process in the medium. And the medium transmits that process at its own intrinsic speed.

The time it takes for the vibration to reach the observer depends on only one thing — the number of aols between the source and the eye. The more aols in the path, the longer the transmission. Direction plays no role, because the medium is the same in all directions. No wind. No drift. No difference between “against the flow” and “with the flow” — because there is no flow.

That is why Michelson detected no difference in the speed of light. Not because the medium does not exist, but because the medium he was looking for behaves completely differently from what he assumed. He was searching for an ether — a moving medium with flows and winds.

But aol space is something fundamentally different: an absolutely homogeneous, isotropic medium, fundamentally at rest, in which the speed of transmission of vibrations is the same in all directions by its very nature.

The Michelson experiment did not refute the existence of a medium. It refuted a specific and incorrect model of that medium.

The Michelson experiment is one of those cases where the aol model offers a more natural explanation than standard physics. Standard physics was forced to introduce the postulate of the constancy of the speed of light and build the entire theory of relativity upon it. In the aol model, the constancy of speed follows naturally from the nature of the medium itself — without additional postulates. The aol model does not generate paradoxes or phenomena. It does not bend space, nor does it slow down time.

Michelson's experiment demonstrated not the absence of a medium, but the limits of the understanding of it at that time. Physics abandoned ether not because it does not exist, but because it did not fit the familiar mechanics of fluids and gases. As a result, the idea of emptiness was accepted — and with it came postulates, paradoxes, and the need to explain phenomena without a carrier. 

The aol model does the opposite: it restores the medium, not as a hypothesis, but as a necessary condition for any process. Light no longer requires “duality,” abstract fields, or postulates. It becomes what it should be in a mechanical universe — the transmission of motion through contact and pressure. And then it becomes clear: the problem was not in the Michelson experiment. The problem was that the wrong reality was being searched for.
